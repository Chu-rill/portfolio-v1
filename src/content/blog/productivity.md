There’s a fine line between preparing to work and avoiding the work itself. And I’ve found myself on the wrong side of that line more times than I’d like to admit.

I recently got a MacBook, and coming from a Linux background, I wanted a near-replica of the setup I was already comfortable with. That led me down a rabbit hole of YouTube videos on the best window managers, plugins, and apps. Suddenly, I’m knee-deep in setups, installing tools, configuring my editor, tweaking dotfiles, testing themes, and reorganizing my system.

For a while, it feels great. Every small change looks like momentum. Every polished detail gives me a sense of control. On the surface, I’m being productive. But beneath it, I’m avoiding the harder part the actual writing of code.

It’s a comforting cycle because it offers instant gratification. You feel accomplished without having done anything. We chase the “ideal setup” that promises peak productivity but often replace doing the task with endlessly getting ready to do it.
The truth is that real progress is rarely clean. It’s messy commits, half-baked drafts, failed experiments, and work that doesn’t look polished yet. But in the end, only that messy part moves us forward.

Don’t confuse motion with progress.
